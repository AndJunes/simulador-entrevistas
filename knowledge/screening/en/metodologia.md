## What a recruiter screening call is

A recruiter screen (often called a "phone screen" or "introductory call") is a short early conversation. The recruiter uses it to narrow the candidate pool before the hiring team invests interview time. SHRM describes these calls as short and fairly simple, but says they set the tone for the rest of the process and should be prepared seriously [K08]. Duke's career center advises candidates to know which stage they are in and who they are talking to (recruiter, HR or hiring manager), and to tailor their answers and questions to that person [K38].

What this means for the candidate:
- A recruiter usually checks fit, motivation, communication and logistics.
- They rarely go deep into technical problem solving. That comes in later rounds.
- Answers should be accurate and clear, but not deeply technical.
- ⚠ sin fuente — heurística: assume the recruiter may not be an engineer. Explain technical work in terms of outcomes, not implementation detail.

## What recruiters assess on a screen

SHRM's guidance for recruiters lists these as things to check on a phone screen [K08]:

- **Communication.** Can the candidate hold a conversation easily, or do they sound over-rehearsed? Are the answers direct and relevant? Can they clearly explain their past work and how they used their skills?
- **Motivation and retention.** What does the candidate want, and what motivates them? Why did they leave past roles? Recruiters are told to dig past "location and pay" as reasons.
- **Compensation and location.** Pay expectations and where the candidate lives or wants to work.
- **Qualification and role understanding.** Is the candidate qualified, and do they understand the role?
- **Verification.** Recruiters confirm dates of recent roles. They probe vague skill descriptions and check specific, quantified claims.

These map onto the four areas the target recruiter evaluates:
- English: communication.
- Motivation: motivation and retention.
- Experience: qualification and alignment.
- Logistics: compensation, location, availability.

SHRM also recommends that recruiters use the same questions and a ratings scorecard for every candidate [K08]. Google's structured-interviewing guide likewise rates answers on a standard rubric [K09]. So the candidate is being compared against fixed criteria, not judged only on likability.

## Structure of a 15-minute introductory call

⚠ sin fuente — heurística. No authoritative source gives a minute-by-minute script for a screen. The outline below is a practice template built from the content areas in [K08] and the closing-questions advice in [K13]:

| Block | Approx. time | What happens | Candidate goal |
|---|---|---|---|
| Opening | ~1 min | Greetings, recruiter explains the call | Warm, clear greeting; confirm you can hear well |
| Pitch | ~1–1.5 min | "Tell me about yourself" | 60–90 s answer tailored to the role (see `preguntas.md`) |
| Motivation | ~2 min | Why this company / role, what you know about the product | Show specific research; connect to your goals |
| Experience | ~4–5 min | Walk through a backend project, stack (Node.js/Rust), a bug or incident | Concrete examples with your own role and results |
| Logistics | ~2–3 min | Availability, contract type, time zone, rate expectations, other processes | Clear, honest, prepared numbers and dates |
| Your questions | ~2 min | "Do you have any questions for me?" | 2–3 prepared questions, including next steps |
| Close | ~0.5 min | Next steps | Thank them; confirm the timeline |

In a 15-minute call, one rambling answer can push out the logistics or your own questions. Short, structured answers protect the time the recruiter needs to complete the scorecard.

## What a strong screen looks like for a junior backend role

The O*NET profile for Software Developers (15-1252.00) describes the core of the work [K11]:
- analyzing requirements;
- conferring with engineers about interfaces and performance;
- modifying software to fix errors or improve performance;
- testing;
- reporting status.

It lists speaking, active listening, critical thinking and active learning among the essential skills [K11].

For a junior candidate, a strong screen shows:
- **Relevant hands-on evidence.** One or two backend projects (APIs, databases, services) explained with your role and a result.
- **Learning ability.** How you picked up a new language or tool, for example Rust. This matches "active learning" in [K11] and "Learn and Be Curious" in Amazon's principles [K12].
- **Ownership of bugs and incidents.** Fixing errors is a listed core task [K11].
- **Clear, intelligible English.** The goal is to be understood easily, not to sound native. Research separates accent from intelligibility: a strong accent does not necessarily make speech harder to understand [K20]. The CEFR describes B2 speakers as producing speech with a fairly even tempo and few noticeably long pauses, and C1 speakers as fluent and almost effortless [K19].
- **Prepared logistics.** Availability date, time-zone overlap, contract understanding and a researched rate range [K16].

## How the candidate should prepare

- **Research the company and product** before the call. Asking the recruiter what the company does is a classic mistake (see `errores.md`).
- **Prepare a 60–90 s pitch** [K05][K06] and 3–5 STAR stories that can cover several questions [K01][K02].
- **Prepare numbers.** Find a researched rate or salary range and know your minimum before the call [K16].
- **Prepare questions.** Have 3–5 questions ready, including one about next steps [K13].
- **Practice out loud.** Silent rehearsal does not match the real task (see `errores.md`).
- **Set up for a phone or video call.** Use a quiet place without distractions, and speak in a clear, normal conversational voice [K07].
