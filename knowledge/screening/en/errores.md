## Not researching the company

**The mistake:** you arrive without knowing what the company builds. Then you ask the recruiter what the company does, or give a generic "why us" answer. The UNI career-services list of common interview mistakes includes doing your company research during the interview. Its advice is to research products, size, reputation and history beforehand [K14]. UT Austin's guide says research lets you speak intelligently about how you could contribute [K07].

**Detection signal:** the motivation answer has no company-specific noun (product, protocol, client type, mission).

**Fix:**
- Before the call, note 2–3 concrete facts about the product.
- Note one way your experience connects to it.

## Rambling or unfocused answers

**The mistake:** long answers with no clear point, or a point that only appears at the end. UNI lists unfocused, disorganized and rambling responses as a common mistake [K14]. On a 15-minute screen, rambling also eats the time reserved for logistics and your own questions.

**Detection signal:** the answer runs well over the target duration in `form_targets.yaml`. Or the first sentence does not answer the question.

**Fix:**
- Lead with the answer in one sentence, then support it.
- Keep "tell me about yourself" to 60–90 s [K05].

## One-word answers

**The mistake:** answering open questions with "yes", "no" or one short line, which leaves the recruiter with nothing to score. UNI advises against mostly yes/no answers and suggests planning to speak about half the time [K14].

**Fix:** answer, then add one concrete example or detail ("Yes — for example, in my last project I…").

## Speaking negatively about past employers

**The mistake:** complaining about a former boss, team or client when asked why you are leaving or what you didn't like. UNI and UTS Careers both list this [K14][K15]. UTS notes that the negativity tends to attach to the candidate even when the complaint is fair [K15].

**Fix:** frame the move as moving toward something, such as a technology, a domain or more ownership, not away from someone.

## Leading with money, or having no researched number

There are two opposite mistakes.

**Opening with salary demands** before the role is discussed. UNI lists announcing a minimum pay figure near the beginning of the interview [K14].

**Having no researched figure when asked.** Guessing, naming an unexplained number, or refusing to engage. MIT CAPD advises researching market ranges and total compensation before any compensation conversation [K16]. A common strategy is a researched range whose bottom is still acceptable to you [K17].

**Fix:**
- Let the recruiter raise compensation.
- When they do, give a researched range with its unit and currency (hourly or monthly for a contract), and stay open to discussion.

## Having no questions for the recruiter

**The mistake:** saying "No, I think you covered everything." UNI lists this mistake explicitly [K14]. Penn State's career office recommends preparing 3–5 meaningful questions that the website doesn't already answer [K13].

**Fix:** keep 2–3 questions ready, always including the next steps and the timeline [K13].

## Memorized, robotic delivery

**The mistake:** reciting a script word for word, sounding over-rehearsed, or losing your place when you forget a line. SHRM says recruiters check whether candidates converse easily or are overly rehearsed [K08]. UNI lists memorized responses where parts get forgotten [K14]. Texas Tech advises against memorizing a script and recommends a list of key words instead [K05].

**Fix:** memorize the structure and 3–4 key points, not sentences. Practice several versions.

## Vague or inflated claims

**The mistake:** describing skills in general terms ("I know Rust", "I improved performance a lot") or giving numbers you can't back up. SHRM tells recruiters to ask follow-ups when skill descriptions are vague, and to confirm specific, quantifiable claims [K08].

**Fix:**
- Give honest scope and evidence: what you built, for how long, and what changed.
- If you don't know a number, say so instead of inventing one.
- For technical questions you can't answer, reason aloud rather than bluffing [K37].

## Rehearsing silently

**The mistake:** preparing only by reading or thinking through answers. UTS Careers recommends practicing out loud, alone or with someone, because that is what the interview requires [K15]. Spoken delivery problems (pauses, speed, fillers) only show up when you speak.

**Fix:** record full mock answers and review timing and pauses with the simulator.

## Leading with soft skills instead of job skills

**The mistake:** spending the pitch on "I'm a team player and a hard worker". UTS Careers notes that most roles hinge on job-specific knowledge, so candidates should lead with those skills [K15].

**Fix:** open with your backend stack and what you built. Then show collaboration through an example.

## Apologizing for your English or accent

**The mistake:** opening with "Sorry, my English is not very good", or trying to fake a native accent. Research separates accentedness from intelligibility: strongly accented speech can still be highly intelligible and easy to understand [K20]. Perceived fluency is driven mostly by pauses and speaking speed, while self-corrections contribute little [K21].

**Fix:**
- Focus on clear structure, a steady pace and short pauses, not accent.
- If you missed a question, ask the recruiter to repeat it. Don't apologize for your level.
- ⚠ sin fuente — heurística: an unprompted apology frames the conversation around a weakness the recruiter may not have noticed.
